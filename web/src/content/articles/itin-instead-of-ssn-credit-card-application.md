When you sit down to fill out a credit card application and the form asks for your Social Security Number, it can feel like a dead end. For millions of immigrants and foreign nationals living in the United States, though, there is a direct path forward: entering your Individual Taxpayer Identification Number in that same field.

According to the Social Security Administration, issuers cannot legally require you to provide a Social Security number if you do not have one. You can use an ITIN instead, another nine-digit identifier that functions the same way as a Social Security number on a credit application.

This guide explains exactly how the substitution works, where to enter your ITIN, which issuers accept it, and what to watch for so you do not waste a hard inquiry on a card that will reject you at the door.

---

## What exactly is an ITIN, and why does it work on a credit card form?

An Individual Taxpayer Identification Number is a tax processing number the IRS issues to people who are not eligible for a Social Security Number. The IRS created ITINs in 1996 so that people with U.S. tax obligations could comply with tax law regardless of immigration status.

ITINs are nine-digit numbers for people who need a U.S. taxpayer identification number but do not qualify for an SSN. They are mainly used for filing federal tax returns, but they can also stand in for an SSN on a credit card application.

The key detail for credit applications is the format. An ITIN uses the same nine-digit format as a Social Security Number and always begins with the number "9." That means any application field that accepts an SSN can, technically, accept an ITIN too, as long as the issuer's back-end system is configured to allow it.

Banks and credit unions widely recognize an ITIN as a valid identifier for opening accounts and approving credit applications. "Widely" does not mean "universally," though, which is why you need to verify each issuer before applying.

## Where exactly do I put my ITIN on the application form?

*A question we hear often:* readers sometimes assume there is a separate ITIN field hidden somewhere in the form.

There is not. When you apply for a credit card, you will see a field for your Social Security Number. Some issuers allow ITIN holders to enter their number there instead; others do not accept ITIN applications at all.

In practice, you type your nine-digit ITIN exactly where the form says "Social Security Number." Some issuers label the field "SSN or ITIN" to signal that both are accepted. Others label it "SSN" even when they accept an ITIN, which causes confusion.

If you are applying online and the form rejects your ITIN because it starts with 9 and the system flags non-standard SSNs, that is usually a sign the issuer's online portal is not ITIN-friendly. Calling the issuer or visiting a branch often resolves it. Our guide on [applying online vs. in branch with an ITIN](/articles/credit-card-itin-apply-online-vs-in-branch) walks through those scenarios in detail.

## Does using an ITIN instead of an SSN change how issuers evaluate my application?

*Readers frequently ask* whether substituting an ITIN signals something negative to an underwriter.

The short answer is no. The ITIN is a neutral identifier. What actually drives approval decisions is your U.S. credit history, income, and the information tied to that identifier in the credit bureaus' files. Credit bureaus track activity by ITIN, so your file builds the same way it would with an SSN.

The real challenge is not the ITIN itself but the thin file that often comes with it. Many ITIN holders are new to U.S. credit, and a short or nonexistent file makes issuers cautious. Card issuers that accept ITINs may still deny your application if you have no credit history.

The standard path around this is to start with a [secured credit card with an ITIN](/secured-credit-cards), which requires a refundable deposit in exchange for a credit line. Because the deposit limits the issuer's risk, many secured card providers are less strict about application requirements. After 12-24 months of on-time payments, most issuers will consider you for an unsecured card. Our article on [upgrading from secured to unsecured with an ITIN](/articles/upgrade-secured-to-unsecured-credit-card-itin) covers that graduation process.

## How is an ITIN different from an SSN, and does that difference matter for credit cards?

Both numbers are nine digits, but their origins and limits are distinct.

One practical difference: an SSN is a lifelong identifier that stays with you once issued, and you never have to do anything to keep it active. ITINs work differently. You must use yours to file a federal tax return at least once every three years, or it goes inactive.

An ITIN is also for tax purposes only. It does not authorize work, grant immigration status, or provide access to Social Security benefits.

For credit card purposes, the most practical difference is coverage: fewer issuers accept an ITIN than an SSN, and some card types, premium travel cards for example, are harder to access without an established U.S. credit profile. That gap narrows significantly once you have 12 or more months of positive card history tied to your ITIN. Applying for a credit card without a Social Security number is possible, and many of the largest card companies accept ITIN holders regardless of immigration status.

## What if my ITIN is expired? Can I still apply for a card?

Banks verify ITINs at the time of account opening, not annually, so an expired ITIN may still work for some banking purposes. If the bank runs a new verification, or if you are applying for a new account or credit product, an expired ITIN can cause problems. It is best to keep yours active.

In 2026, ITINs with middle digits 85, 86, and 87 are scheduled to expire on December 31. If yours falls in that range, renew before applying for any new credit card. The renewal is free using Form W-7 with the renewal box checked, and your ITIN number does not change. The IRS simply refreshes the active status; any credit accounts or financial records tied to your ITIN do not need to be updated.

## What documents should I have ready alongside my ITIN when I apply?

When applying for a credit card with an ITIN, you may be asked for your full legal name, date of birth, and address, along with proof of income, employment status, and foreign credit history.

In addition to your ITIN, gather these before you start the application:

- A government-issued photo ID (a passport is the most universally accepted)
- Proof of U.S. address (a utility bill, lease, or bank statement works at most issuers)
- Proof of income (pay stubs, an offer letter, or your most recent tax return)
- Your ITIN assignment notice (IRS letter CP565) if the issuer asks for verification

Having these ready means you can complete an in-branch application in a single visit rather than two. If you want a full walkthrough of the application steps, our [how to apply for a credit card with an ITIN](/articles/how-to-apply-for-credit-card-with-itin) guide covers each stage.

## 5 FAQs about using an ITIN instead of an SSN on credit card applications

**Can I enter my ITIN in the SSN field on a credit card application?**
Yes. When an issuer accepts an ITIN, you type your 9-digit ITIN directly into the SSN field. Some issuers label the field "SSN/ITIN" to make this clear. Always confirm the specific card accepts an ITIN before applying, because a denial still results in a hard inquiry on your file.

**Is an ITIN the same as an SSN for credit card purposes?**
Not exactly. Both are 9-digit identifiers, but an SSN comes from the Social Security Administration while an ITIN comes from the IRS. Issuers that accept both treat them the same way during identity and credit-history verification. The difference is that not every issuer accepts an ITIN, and an ITIN does not carry the same work-authorization signal as an SSN.

**Which major credit card issuers accept an ITIN instead of an SSN?**
Capital One, American Express, Bank of America, Discover, and many credit unions openly accept an ITIN. Chase and Citi policies vary by card. Check the specific card page or call the issuer before submitting an application.

**Will using an ITIN hurt my credit card approval odds?**
Using an ITIN does not automatically hurt your odds, but a thin or nonexistent U.S. credit file often does. Many ITIN holders start with a secured card to build history, then graduate to unsecured cards after 12-24 months. The ITIN itself is a neutral identifier, not a red flag.

**What happens to my credit card if my ITIN expires?**
Your existing cards and credit history stay intact. An expired ITIN does not cancel open accounts. Applying for a new card with an expired ITIN can cause verification failures, though, so renew via Form W-7 before submitting any new applications.
