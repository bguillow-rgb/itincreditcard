Prepaid Card vs. Secured Credit Card for ITIN Holders

By Editorial Staff · Secured & Starter Cards · Published August 21, 2026

Advertiser disclosure:ITIN Credit Card is an independent resource. Some links on this page are from partners who compensate us, which may affect which products we feature and where. This does not change our editorial guidance and is never a cost to you. See our full disclosure.

Quick Answer

Prepaid cards let you spend your own money but report nothing to credit bureaus, so they cannot build a U.S. credit history. Secured credit cards require a refundable deposit, work like a real credit card, and report to all three bureaus. ITIN holders who want to build credit should choose a secured card.

If you just arrived in the U.S. and need a way to pay for things without cash, it is easy to confuse prepaid cards and secured credit cards. They look nearly identical in your wallet. The difference, though, is enormous: one builds a U.S. credit history and one does not. For ITIN holders, picking the wrong product can cost you 12-24 months of credit-building time before you even realize what happened.

Wait, aren’t prepaid cards and secured cards basically the same thing?

A question we hear often: they are not the same, and the distinction matters enormously for anyone trying to establish U.S. credit.

A prepaid card works like a rechargeable gift card. You load money onto it, spend down the balance, and reload it. The card issuer never extends you credit, never checks your credit file, and never reports your payment behavior to Equifax, Experian, or TransUnion.

A secured credit card is a genuine line of credit backed by a refundable cash deposit. It reports to the credit bureaus. A prepaid card does not. Most secured card issuers do not require a minimum credit score, and some, like OpenSky, skip the hard credit check entirely. Every on-time payment gets reported to the bureaus, slowly building the credit file that landlords, lenders, and employers may check later.

If your goal is to build U.S. credit, a prepaid card is the wrong tool entirely.

Can I really get a secured credit card with just an ITIN?

Yes. You do not need a Social Security number. Many issuers accept an ITIN in place of an SSN for credit card applications, including Chase, Bank of America, American Express, Capital One, and Citi.

For secured cards specifically, the ITIN-friendly options are broad. Capital One accepts ITIN applications for several cards, including the Capital One Platinum Secured Card, and has a reputation for being accessible to immigrants. Citi accepts ITINs as well, particularly if you already have a banking relationship with them; the Citi Secured Mastercard is a solid option for ITIN holders starting from zero.

If you have no U.S. credit history and want the easiest entry point, OpenSky is worth a look. The OpenSky Secured Visa accepts valid ID without an SSN and skips the hard credit pull. See our full guide to secured credit cards for ITIN holders for a closer look at every major issuer.

What does a prepaid card actually cost compared to a secured card?

This one comes up a lot: many people assume prepaid cards are cheaper because there is no credit check and no deposit required. The cost comparison is more complicated than that.

Add up monthly fees and reload charges and many prepaid cards run $100-$150 per year, with nothing to show for it in your credit file. A secured card’s deposit is not really a cost; you get it back. The annual fee is the only real expense, and several secured cards carry no annual fee at all. Over two years, a secured card is almost always the better financial choice for an ITIN holder who plans to stay in the U.S.

I already have a prepaid card. Should I switch?

Readers frequently ask: whether they should keep their prepaid card or cut it up. It depends on your situation.

Keep the prepaid card if you genuinely only need it for spending control and you already have a secured card doing the credit-building work. Some ITIN holders use a prepaid card as a budgeting tool (spending only what they load) while using a separate secured card for purchases they pay off in full each month. That combination works fine.

Drop the prepaid card, or at minimum open a secured card alongside it, if your only U.S. payment product is the prepaid card. The path is straightforward: get an ITIN, open a secured card with a bureau-reporting issuer, pay on time every month. That’s it.

The longer you wait to open a bureau-reporting account, the longer your credit file sits at zero. A scoreable credit file typically takes 3-6 months to establish with on-time payments. Getting to a 700+ score from zero generally takes 12-18 months.

Does any prepaid card report to the credit bureaus for ITIN holders?

Standard prepaid cards do not. The Visa and Mastercard prepaid products sold at grocery stores and pharmacies are payment tools only; they generate no credit file activity under any circumstances.

A small number of hybrid products blur this line. Some fintech “credit builder” cards work by locking a portion of your deposit and reporting your payment behavior to the bureaus. The Current Build Card, for example, lets you load money and spend from that balance (no interest, since you’re spending your own money) while also reporting to bureaus and earning rewards on dining and groceries. These hybrid products are technically distinct from traditional prepaid cards, even if the mechanics feel similar.

The safest rule: before assuming any card reports to the bureaus, ask the issuer directly. The question to ask is: “Does this card report my payment activity to Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion?” If the answer is anything other than a clear yes, treat it as a non-reporting product. Our guide to which ITIN credit cards report to all three bureaus goes through the issuer-by-issuer picture.

What do I need to apply for a secured card with an ITIN?

A question we hear often: the document requirements vary by issuer but are generally straightforward.

Most ITIN-accepting secured card issuers will ask for:

  • Your ITIN (9-digit number starting with 9, from IRS Form W-7)
  • A government-issued photo ID (a passport is universally accepted)
  • A U.S. mailing address
  • Proof of income (pay stubs, bank statements, or a letter of employment)
  • A U.S. bank account for the security deposit (not required by every issuer)

Part-time work qualifies as income at many issuers. You generally need to be at least 18 with a U.S. mailing address, and no prior credit history or SSN is required.

If you do not yet have a U.S. bank account, OpenSky stands out because it allows you to fund your deposit via money order. For everyone else, opening a basic checking account first (possible with an ITIN at most banks and credit unions) will make the secured card application smoother.

For a step-by-step walkthrough of the full application process, see how to apply for a credit card with an ITIN.

Which secured card should an ITIN holder choose as their first card?

The right answer depends on your situation, but a few filters narrow it down quickly.

No credit check needed: OpenSky Secured Visa accepts ITIN, skips the hard pull, and does not require a bank account. It charges an annual fee (currently around $35) but is the most accessible entry point for someone with zero U.S. credit history.

Major bank relationship: If you already bank with Capital One, Bank of America, Citi, or Wells Fargo, start there. An existing checking or savings account with the bank improves your approval odds, and their secured cards are reasonable starting points for ITIN holders with no U.S. credit history.

No annual fee: The Wells Fargo Active Cash Card carries a $0 annual fee, a cash rewards sign-up bonus, and accepts an ITIN in place of an SSN. It does require some existing credit history, so it suits ITIN holders who have already established at least a thin file.

Graduation path: Many newcomers reach a usable FICO score in 3-6 months with consistent on-time payments; a score of 700 or higher typically takes 12-24 months. Look for issuers that explicitly offer a path from secured to unsecured. Our article on upgrading from a secured to an unsecured card with an ITIN explains how that transition typically works.
